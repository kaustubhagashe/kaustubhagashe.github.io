The Impossible Number Game

What’s the mathematical trick?

After each guess, the game removes that number and keeps the larger of the two remaining intervals. If your guess is outside the current possible interval, it gives the truthful clue without shrinking the interval. After the sixth clue, it randomly selects a number from the surviving interval. That number was never guessed, and every HIGHER/LOWER clue agrees with it.

The Eight Queens Puzzle

Place eight queens on the chessboard so that no two queens attack each other — no two may share a row, a column or a diagonal. Click a square to place a queen, click a queen to take it back, or drag a queen from the tray.

There are 92 solutions in all. “Show answer” displays one of them.

How can it be solved?

1 · Use the rules to cut the search

Eight queens means exactly one queen in every row and every column. Instead of choosing 8 squares out of 64 (4,426,165,368 ways), you only need to decide which column the queen of each row goes in: at most 8! = 40,320 arrangements.

2 · Place row by row and backtrack

Put a queen in the top row, then in the next row choose the first column that is not attacked by the queens already placed. If a row has no safe square, step back, move the previous queen one column along, and continue. This “backtracking” search finds all solutions after visiting just 2,057 partial boards.

3 · Keep track of attacks

Two squares share a diagonal when row − column is the same, or row + column is the same. A program only needs three lists — used columns, used “\” diagonals, used “/” diagonals — to test a square instantly. By hand, cross out every square a new queen attacks.

4 · Count the answers

The search finds 92 solutions. Rotating or reflecting the board turns one solution into another, so only 12 are truly different; every other solution is a rotated or mirrored copy of one of those 12.

The Knight’s Tour

Click any square to place the knight, then keep moving it in L-shapes — two squares one way and one square at a right angle — so that it lands on every one of the 64 squares exactly once. Your route is traced with a line as you go.

“Show answer” traces a closed tour from your starting square: the last square is one knight’s move away from the first, so the route forms a loop.

How can it be solved?

1 · Warnsdorff’s rule

Always move to the square from which the knight would have the fewest onward moves. Hard-to-reach squares are visited first, before they get cut off. This simple rule solves the tour from most starting squares without any backtracking.

2 · Stay near the edges early

Corners have only two exits and edge squares only three or four. Visit these awkward squares early; the centre squares keep many exits and can wait until the end.

3 · Open and closed tours

If the last square is a knight’s move from the first, the tour is closed: it is a loop that can be started from any square. Because the loop passes through all 64 squares, “Show answer” can start the same loop wherever you placed your knight.

4 · Stuck? Backtrack

If the knight has no unvisited square to go to before 64 squares are done, undo a few moves and choose another route. A computer does the same, trying alternatives until the tour is complete. There are 26,534,728,821,064 directed closed tours on an 8 × 8 board.

Colour trick: a knight always changes square colour with every move. So after 63 moves it stands on the opposite colour from where it began — and a square a knight’s move from the start is always of that opposite colour, which is why a closed loop through all 64 squares can exist.

The Tower of Hanoi

Move the whole stack of rings from the Source tower to the Target tower, one ring at a time. Choose how many towers and rings you want to play with.

3 – 5 towers
Moves made

Rules of the game

  1. All the rings start stacked on the Source tower (the first one), largest at the bottom and smallest on top.
  2. Your aim is to rebuild the same stack, in the same order, on the Target tower (the last one).
  3. Move one ring at a time, and only the top ring of a tower. Click a tower to lift its top ring, then click the tower where it should go.
  4. A larger ring may never be placed on a smaller ring.
  5. With the extra towers you can park rings temporarily, so 4 or 5 towers need fewer moves than 3 towers. For 3 towers the minimum is 2n − 1 moves for n rings; for more towers the best-known (Frame–Stewart) numbers are shown.

If the ring you pick has nowhere to go, or no move is possible at all, you will see a deadlock message — use “Undo move” or “Start over”.
